## Biography

Hiram Sams walks out of the record on the first of April, 1864, and never walks
back in. Up to that point his life is only faintly sketched: born in the hill
country of Sullivan County, Tennessee, around 1830; married to Jane Carrier;
father of at least two daughters. Then, in the spring of 1862, he enrolled as a
private in a Tennessee regiment fighting for the Confederacy — and within a year
he was gone from the ranks, marked absent without leave, and by April 1864
formally branded a deserter.

Whether he was killed, kept running, or quietly started over somewhere under
another name, no one ever learned. There is no grave, no pension file, no later
census line. Back home his absence was measured in the county's account books: his
wife and children drew poor relief through the war years, the telltale mark of a
household that had lost the person who fed it.

His daughter Harriet grew up telling of Cherokee blood in the family. It is
possible — no more than possible — that the story began with Hiram himself, said
to a small girl before he marched away and vanished, and never afterward explained
or taken back.

## Research Notes

Hiram Sams was born about 1830–33 in Sullivan County, Tennessee. He enrolled on
14 May 1862 at Zollicoffer as a private in Company F of the 63rd Tennessee
Infantry — Fain's Regiment — in Confederate service. He went absent without
leave on 1 June 1863 and was formally declared a deserter on 1 April
1864.[^csa_63rd_tn_service]

What his absence cost at home is visible in the county's own accounts: the
family drew indigent relief across 1862–64, the pattern of a household that has
lost the person earning its living.[^indigent_relief_1862_1864] No occupation
was ever recorded for him.

Nothing survives after April 1864 — no death record, no pension file, no later
census entry. Whether he died, kept moving, or simply left no further paper is
not recoverable from the record as it stands. He is named on his daughter
Harriet's 1913 Virginia death certificate as "Hiram Samms".[^sams_death_cert_1913]
His probable father is Obediah Sams, whose own presence is well documented by the
1850 census and by fifty years of deeds.[^census_1850_sullivan_tn] His widow Jane
appears to have remarried into a Booher family by 1880, which is why their
daughter Harriet was later known as "Harriet Booher".[^census_1880_booher]

This branch has been searched and cleared of both the Cherokee and the African
questions raised elsewhere in the family: no Cherokee roll carries any of these
surnames,[^cherokee_roll_searches] and no slave schedule records any holding by
these ancestors.[^slave_schedule_searches]

One untestable idea is worth naming rather than leaving unsaid: the family's
Cherokee tradition may trace to Hiram himself, as a claim of Indian ancestry made
to his young daughter Harriet before he marched off in 1862 and vanished — the
kind of parting statement that hardens into lore because the person who made it
never comes back to qualify it. Nothing in the record speaks to it either way,
and whether Hiram's own belief rested on anything is beyond recovery.
