## Biography

Harriet Eliza Sams was born on a summer day in August 1856, in the hill country of
Sullivan County, Tennessee. Her childhood was shaped by the Civil War and by the
absence it left behind: her father, Hiram, went off to the war when she was still
a small girl and never came home. He was gone by the time she was eight, and no
one in the family ever learned what became of him.

With her father lost and her mother, Jane, left on her own, the household did not
hold together. Harriet and her sisters were parceled out to relatives, and for a
stretch of her girlhood she lived in the home of an aunt and uncle. Her mother
eventually remarried into a family named Booher, and Harriet grew up so closely
tied to that household that people around her simply called her "Harriet Booher"
— a name that stuck to her for years, right through the time a young man named
George Lewis was courting her. George wrote her adopted name down as he heard it
— "Boar" — in the family Bible he kept.

In the spring of 1882, in Washington County, Virginia, Harriet married George W.
Lewis. She settled into life there as a wife and mother, raising a family that
included sons and daughters — among them a boy she named James Hiram Lewis, giving
him the name of the father who had vanished decades before. The family kept a few
stories about her that never made it into any official record: a tradition that
she had Cherokee, or perhaps Creek, ancestry, and a more colorful tale that she
had first come to the little community of Friendship, Virginia, as a housemaid for
the local storekeeper, brought there as a bride around the time of a summer
camp meeting on Rush Creek.

Harriet spent the rest of her life in the Holston district of Washington County.
She died there in January 1913, of tuberculosis, at the age of 56, and was buried
the next day at Friendship Cemetery in Glade Spring. It was George, her husband of
thirty years, who gave the details for her death record — a last act of knowing
her better than anyone.

## Research Notes

Harriet Eliza Sams was born 4 August 1856 in Sullivan County, Tennessee, and
married George W. Lewis in the spring of 1882 in Washington County, Virginia —
the register entry itself has never been located, though the marriage is
corroborated independently elsewhere.[^wash_co_va_marriage_1882] She died of
tuberculosis of the trachea on 4
January 1913 in the Holston district of Washington County, Virginia, aged 56
years, 5 months and 0 days, and was buried the next day at Friendship Cemetery,
Glade Spring.[^sams_death_cert_1913]

Her death certificate has now been read, and it is the anchor for the whole Sams
line. It names her parents as "Hiram Samms" and "Jane Carrol", spellings that
resolve to Hiram Sams and Jane Carrier, and gives her occupation as housewife and
her race as white. The informant was "Geo Lewis" of Glade Spring — her husband,
George W. Lewis, which is as close to first-hand as this kind of record gets for
her own details.[^sams_death_cert_1913] She is recorded as white on every
document that states a race — her own 1913 death certificate and the 1949
delayed birth certificate of her daughter Bettie
Jane.[^sams_death_cert_1913][^delayed_birth_bettie_lewis_1949]

Two things on the death certificate are worth drawing out. Her stated age, 56
years 5 months 0 days on 4 January 1913, agrees exactly with a birth on 4 August
1856, so the two halves of the document check each other. And her birthplace in
Sullivan County, Tennessee, fits the 1880 census that finds her there as a niece
in another household.[^census_1880_sullivan_harriet]

Against that, her husband gave the birthplaces of both her parents as "Not
Known".[^sams_death_cert_1913] Whatever George knew of the Sams family, he did not
know where either of them came from — which is part of why the generation above
Hiram and Jane has been so hard to reach.

The 1870 census shows her mother Jane widowed and living with her own mother and
stepfather, with her daughters not in the household[^census_1870_carrier_berry] —
a family broken up and its children placed with relatives. That she named a son
James Hiram Lewis, apparently for her father, and that her son Walter's 1955
Tennessee death record gives her maiden name as Sams, both point the same
way.[^lewis_death_cert_walter_1955] Walter's own Social Security application has
been read directly, but it does not carry a mother's-maiden-name field; it
corroborates his identity by birth date and place rather than by naming Sams,
and no equivalent record has been checked for his siblings.[^numident_lewis_children]

The family's Ancestry tree gives her death as 1914. Her death certificate gives 4
January 1913, and is the better authority; the 1914 date has not been
carried.[^sams_death_cert_1913]

## The two surnames

She appears in the record under two maiden names, Sams and Booher, and the
current reading is that both are accurate — they describe different things.

**Sams** is the birth name. Her 1913 death certificate names her father as "Hiram
Samms",[^sams_death_cert_1913] her son Walter's 1955 Tennessee death record gives
"Harriet Sams",[^lewis_death_cert_walter_1955] and she named a son James Hiram
Lewis.

**Booher** — rendered variously Bohr, Boar and Boher — is the name she was known
by locally. The 1949 delayed birth certificate of her daughter Bettie Jane Lewis
gives the mother's full maiden name as "Harriett Booher"; it is a sworn,
notarized statement by Mrs. Flora Mae Greever, who cites a family Bible said to
have been printed in 1872 and examined directly by the
notary.[^delayed_birth_bettie_lewis_1949] The handwritten Bible-transcription
notes say the same, and are candid about the spellings: one page records that
"Harriet Boar was in Old Bible" while "Harret Bohr" is the form that "appears more
often in outside records".[^bible_notes_lewis] The old Bible carried entries in
George W. Lewis's own hand.[^bible_notes_lewis] If the "Boar" line is among them,
it is his own spelling of his wife's name, set down by the man who later informed
her 1913 death certificate.

The reconciliation, worked out here and not yet tested against further records,
runs through her mother. After Hiram Sams deserted in 1864 and was never traced
again, Jane appears to have remarried into a Booher family: the 1880 census places
a Jane Booher, step-mother, aged 40 and born in Tennessee, in the Kinderhook
District household of Jacob H. Booher, with a 16-year-old Elizabeth Samms — a
younger Sams daughter, not Harriet — still in the house.[^census_1880_booher] On
this reading Harriet was born a Sams but grew up in or alongside her mother's
Booher household and was genuinely known as "Harriet Booher" through the years
George Lewis courted her, so the 1913 certificate reports the birth-family facts
and the 1949 one her lived name. Neither an adoption nor a fostering by strangers
is needed to explain the name.

## The Ike Ingram story

A separate handwritten note offers a more dramatic account: that Harriet "was a
bride at the June meeting on Rush Creek" and was "brought to Friendship, VA by Ike
Ingram, a storekeeper, as a housemaid/servant", and that she said she was of "a
tribe of Cherokee or Creek Indian".[^bible_notes_lewis] A real I. H. Ingram, a
merchant born about 1852, is in the 1880 census at Holston in Washington County,
matching the initials, trade and locale, and he is buried at Friendship Cemetery
as Harriet was.[^census_1880_ingram] But no record places Harriet in his
household — his 1880 household is a wife and two young daughters with no servants
— and the Jane-remarried-into-Booher explanation accounts for the same gap with
far less strain. The Ingram version is kept here as a recorded piece of family
tradition, not as a finding; it may be a later, more vivid retelling of the same
vaguer story.

## The Cherokee tradition

The old family Bible records a tradition of Cherokee — or, in its own uncertain
phrasing, "Cherokee or Creek" — ancestry.[^bible_notes_lewis] No document
supports it: no Sams, Samms, Carrier, Carroll, Booher or Roark name appears on any
Cherokee roll despite a real financial incentive to file a claim in
1906–09,[^cherokee_roll_searches] and every generation of this family is recorded
white.

This is the side of the family the spoken tradition actually points to. Colleen
Wallick — who kept the Bible and its transcription pages — placed the Native
American ancestry on her mother's *paternal* line, running up through Lorene's
father James Hiram Lewis to his mother, Harriet; her granddaughter Holly recalls
Colleen saying the same. The handwritten Bible notes, thought to be partly in
Colleen's own hand, carry the "Cherokee or Creek" line on this
side.[^bible_notes_lewis] An earlier version of this history attached the
tradition to Alice Louise Stovall instead, from a grandson's misremembering of
Colleen's words; that has been corrected, and it does not change any of the
conclusions reached about Alice.

Harriet was also, for a time, the candidate a later researcher — the same
grandson — reached independently, on the grounds that her birth and parentage
were the hardest in this tree to trace, so a hidden ancestor could most easily
sit there. That reasoning has since fallen away: the difficulty turned out to be
the Sams/Booher name confusion set out above, not a buried origin, and once the
names are reconciled Harriet's descent from Hiram Sams and Jane Carrier is
documented and unremarkable. The family tradition on this side is untouched by
that — it never rested on the gap in the first place, and the old Bible
transcription, if anything, is a point in its favour.

One untestable possibility, raised here only to name it: the tradition may go back
to Hiram Sams himself, who could have told Harriet — then a child of five to eight
— that the family had Indian ancestry before he left for the war in 1862 and
vanished. A parting claim from a father who never came back is the kind of thing
that hardens into unquestioned lore. Whether Hiram's own belief rested on anything
is beyond recovery.
