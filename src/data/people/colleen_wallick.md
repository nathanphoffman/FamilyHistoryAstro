## Biography

Nearly every story in this family history reached the present through Colleen
Wallick. The eldest child of Urie John Wallick and Lorene Lewis, born in the
summer of 1935, she was the one who kept the old family Bible and its loose
transcription pages, and the one who carried the spoken memory forward — including
the account of Native American ancestry. Her own handwritten notes, thought to
date from a time when her memory was sound, point to her mother's *father's* side
of the house: the Lewis–Sams line, and specifically her great-grandmother Harriet
Eliza Sams. Later in life, asked about it directly, she told a different story.

Two threads of her memory disagree about where the ancestry runs, and neither is
simply a mistake. The earlier notes point to Harriet. But in her later years, both
her grandson Nate and her granddaughter Holly independently recall her placing it
on her own *maternal* grandmother's side instead — Nate asked her directly, and she
could not recall the name (Alice Louise Stovall) but felt it was "generally in
that area of the family"; Holly recalls the same direction, naming Maggie Stovall,
who is in fact Alice's mother, a generation further back on the same branch. Set
against her own earlier notes, that later account is treated as the less reliable
of the two — but it was real testimony in its own right, and for years it was what
sent the family looking at Alice instead. Whether the tradition ever proves out, it
is Colleen's telling of it, in both its forms, that gave the generations after her
something to go looking for.

## Research Notes

Colleen Wallick was the eldest child of Urie John Wallick and Lorene E. Lewis.
The family's Ancestry tree gives her birth as 30 July 1935 at Dover,
Pennsylvania, narrowing what had been recorded here only as 1935 or
1936.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

The 1950 census supports it, within limits. It records her as Colleen A.
Wallick, daughter, aged 14 in April 1950, born in Pennsylvania — which puts her
birth between April 1935 and April 1936, exactly where the tree puts
it.[^census_1950_york] What the census cannot do is confirm the day: 30 July, and
Dover as the place, still rest on the tree alone.

She is the source of the family's account of its Native American ancestry, though
her own testimony took two different forms. Her handwritten notes on the old
family Bible — thought to be partly in her own hand, and to date from a time when
her memory was sound — carry the "Cherokee or Creek" tradition on her mother's
*paternal* side: the Lewis–Sams line running up through Lorene's father, James
Hiram Lewis, to his mother Harriet Eliza Sams.[^bible_notes_lewis]

Those notes are not the whole of her testimony, though, and the two threads
disagree. In her later years, both her grandson Nate and her granddaughter Holly
independently recall her placing the ancestry on her own *maternal* grandmother's
side instead. Nate asked her directly; she could not recall the name — Alice
Louise Stovall — but felt it was "generally in that area of the family." Holly
recalls the same direction, naming Maggie Stovall, who is in fact Alice's mother,
one generation further back on the same branch — suggesting the two generations
were conflated in memory rather than that the maternal-line claim itself was
invented. Weighed against Colleen's own earlier notes, this later verbal account is
treated as the less reliable of the two, but it is family testimony in its own
right, and it is what sent much of the early research to Alice's line instead. The
conclusions drawn about Alice's parentage stand on their own evidence and are
unaffected by any of this.
