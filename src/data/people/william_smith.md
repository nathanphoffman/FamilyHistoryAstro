## Biography

William Smith may belong in this family, and he may not. He was certainly a real
man: born in the Glade Spring District of Washington County, Virginia, around
1863, the son of Andrew and Sallie Smith, working as a laborer, married to a woman
named Virgie and raising a son and a niece by the turn of the century. His parents
were recorded as Black and Mulatto; he himself was recorded as Black in 1900 —
and as white on his own 1886 marriage record.

He enters this family's story only as a candidate — the possible father of Alice
Stovall, who was born nearby, at about the right time, with no father ever named
for her. Whether the man who married Jennie Hall in 1886 as a white man is this
same William Smith, or a different one entirely, is unresolved and worth more
digging: nothing here confirms him as Alice's father, but the case against him is
weaker than it first looked. For now he stands just off the edge of the family:
the right age, the right place, and nothing solid joining him to it either way.

## Research Notes

William "Will" Smith appears in the 1870 census of the Glade Spring District as
the son of Andrew Smith, recorded as Black, and Sallie Smith, recorded as
Mulatto.[^census_1870_smith] He is there again in 1880 at
sixteen,[^census_1880_smith] and in 1900 as a labourer of thirty-seven, race
recorded as Black, heading a household with his wife Virgie, their son Richard
born 1889, and a niece, Irene Dickison, living with
them.[^census_1900_smith] The extended household was an ordinary arrangement for
the time and place.

His age places him within the window in which Alice Louise Stovall was
conceived, in the district where Maggie Stovall was living, which is why he was
raised as a candidate for Alice's unrecorded father.

That identification looked weaker once an 1886 marriage record turned up for a
William Smith — named as the son of Andrew and Sallie Smith, the same parents —
recording both parties as white.[^marriage_1886_smith_hall] Taken at face value,
a man recorded Black in 1870 and 1900 cannot be the same man recorded white in
1886, and on that reading the marriage record either belongs to a second,
unrelated Andrew-and-Sallie-Smith couple in the same small district, or an online
tree has merged two different men.

That reading deserves a second look, for two reasons. First, this tree already
documents, on Alice Louise Stovall's own page, that inconsistent racial
recording — white on one document, Black or Mulatto on another — was a common and
expected pattern for people of mixed ancestry in this region under the Racial
Integrity Act era, not necessarily evidence of two different people. Second, a
family photograph of Alice shows her presenting as white; if Will Smith is her
father, a man of purely African ancestry sits awkwardly with that, but a man
capable of being recorded as white in at least one context — as the 1886 groom
was — fits it well. So the race mismatch, rather than ruling him out, may be the
ordinary signature of the same mixed-ancestry man moving between two
classifications depending on the clerk and the context.

None of this confirms the identification; it only reopens a case the earlier
reading had closed too quickly. This needs more digging before it goes anywhere:
his 1880 census entry is cited[^census_1880_smith] but has never actually been
pulled or read for a race field, and a third data point there — Black or Mulatto
again, or white, or something else — would help decide between "one inconsistently
recorded man" and "two different men who happened to share parents' names." No
document has ever tied him directly to Alice or Maggie Stovall at all; the whole
identification still rests on age, district, and the secondhand "Smith" maiden
name from two of Alice's children's SS-5 applications.
