## Biography

More words have been spent arguing about Margaret Hubbell than about almost anyone
else in this family, and hardly any of them concern her life. Of that, only the
outline survives: born somewhere around the middle of the 1700s, married to a man
named Adam Surber in Washington County, Virginia, near the century's end, and dead
by 1822 in Pulaski County, Kentucky — where she and her husband finished their
lives, while the daughter this family descends from stayed behind in Virginia.

What makes her name matter is where it sits. Through her, the family tree reaches
its longest and grandest branch, back through colonial Connecticut and eventually
to Zürich. But the dates refuse to line up cleanly: depending on which year she
was truly born, she is either the mother of that Virginia daughter or she is not,
and the whole ornate upper half of the tree either belongs to this family or to
someone else's. A single marriage document, still sitting unread in an archive,
would decide it.

She came south, at any rate, with an entire New England clan — parents and kin
dying within a few years of one another in the same Holston River valley the rest
of this family sprang from. Whatever the paperwork finally says, these transplanted
Yankees did put down roots in the same Virginia ground.

## Research Notes

Margaret Hubbell is given by the family's Ancestry tree as the wife of Adam
Newell Surber.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

Her parentage is the hardest question in this branch, and it has been argued
back and forth more than once. This is where it currently stands.

## The arithmetic

The tree gives her mother as Waitstill Bishop, born 1746 and married to Justus
Hubbell in 1766 at Salem, New York — which would make Waitstill eleven when
Margaret was born in 1757, and would place the marriage nine years after the
birth.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

There is a second break underneath it. Margaret's marriage to Adam Newell Surber
is dated 1797, in Washington County, Virginia, while their daughter Margaret
Surber — the person this chart actually descends through — is born in 1786,
eleven years earlier.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

## The geography

Against that, the tree puts the whole Bishop family in southwest Virginia at
exactly the moment of that marriage. Waitstill dies in the Holston River valley,
Washington County, in 1797; her parents Joseph Bishop and Sarah Bouton die at
St. Clair's Bottom in the same county in 1798 and
1799.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

Three deaths and a marriage in one small county inside three years is not the
shape of an accidental graft. It is what a family that migrated together looks
like, and it puts these New England people in the same valley the Lewis and
Stovall families come from. Whatever else is wrong here, "a Connecticut family
with no business in Virginia" is not the explanation, and an earlier draft of
this record leaned on that reading longer than the evidence supported.

What carries the strain instead are the two fields nothing corroborates: her
birth year of 1757, and her birthplace of Culpeper — northern Piedmont, some 250
miles from Washington County, and eleven years before her parents'
marriage.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]

## Three readings, and the document that separates them

1. **1757 is right.** The 1797 marriage is a duplicate event of the kind this
   tree throws off elsewhere, Margaret is Justus's daughter by an earlier wife,
   and Waitstill is simply not her mother.
2. **1757 is wrong**, and she was born in the later 1760s or 1770s. Waitstill is
   her mother and the migration is real — but then Margaret Surber, born 1786,
   cannot be her daughter, and this chart's descent leaves the Hubbells at Adam
   Surber's first wife instead.
3. **Two women named Margaret Hubbell** have been merged into one record.

The 1786 date is the one thing that cannot give way. Margaret Surber's son Philip
Isaac Rouse is born in 1808, so moving her birth past the 1797 marriage would
make her a mother at ten.

The document that decides it is the 1797 Washington County marriage bond, in
Virginia's Select Marriages — the same free collection that holds the 1905 Lewis
register. A bond names the bride's father or her consenting parent. If it names
Justus, the first reading dies and the branch is largely vindicated; if it names
anyone else, the graft is exposed. Its form helps as well, since consent was
required only under twenty-one: a bond taken without consent argues against a
birth in the mid-1770s, and one taken with it argues hard against 1757, when she
would have been forty.

Until that bond is read the mother's slot stays empty — not because Waitstill is
disproved, but because the three readings cannot be told apart, and filling the
slot would pick one of them by default.

Her father's slot is kept on the thinnest of grounds: Justus, born 1732, would
have been twenty-five in 1757, which at least holds together.

She and her husband both died in Pulaski County, Kentucky, while the daughter
this chart descends through stayed behind in Virginia.

One caution about the surrounding records. They are visibly corrupt in places:
Waitstill Green is recorded marrying two men on the same day in 1707, and Joseph
Bishop and Sarah Bouton are recorded marrying each other twice, twenty years
apart.[^ancestry_tree_35916700]
