## Biography

The same misfortune caught Jane Carrier three times over. She was born in
Tennessee around 1840 with no father's name attached to hers — the daughter of
Mary Carrier and no one the record will name. She married Hiram Sams, and when he
disappeared into the war her own household came apart the same way: her two young
daughters were sent to live in other people's homes because she could no longer
keep them. And her daughter Harriet, in turn, grew up among relatives and
stepfamily rather than with her own parents.

The 1870 census calls Jane a widow, though the truth was blurrier than that. Her
husband had deserted the army and simply never returned, and "widow" was as much
what she hoped, or what she was willing to say aloud, as anything she knew for
certain. She was living then in her mother's house — her mother having also
remarried after being left on her own.

Later, Jane married again herself, into a family named Booher — and was
widowed a second time before 1880, when she is recorded as head of her own
household. That is how her daughter Harriet came to be known around the county
for decades as "Harriet Booher," a name that was never hers by birth but stuck
to her just the same. In 1889 Jane married a third time, to a widower named
James L. Booher, who happened to live two doors down from her in that same 1880
census.

## Research Notes

Jane Carrier was born about 1840 in Tennessee, the illegitimate daughter of Mary
Carrier; no father is recorded for her. She appears on her daughter Harriet's
1913 Virginia death certificate under the garbled spelling "Jane
Carrol".[^sams_death_cert_1913]

Her own household did not survive the war. By 1870 she is recorded as widowed
and living in the household of her mother Mary and her stepfather George
Berry — Mary having remarried after 1866.[^census_1870_carrier_berry] The
"widowed" is worth reading carefully: her husband Hiram Sams deserted in 1864 and
was never traced again, so the designation may reflect what she believed, what
she was willing to say, or simply the enumerator's shorthand for a woman with no
husband present.

Her daughters Harriet and Mary were not with her in 1870; both had been placed
out.[^census_1870_carrier_berry] Boarding children elsewhere was a common
response to hardship, and here it marks a household that had lost its earner and
could not keep its children.

She remarried into a Booher family afterward and was widowed a second time by
1880: that census records her as Jane Booher, 40, white, widowed, head of her
own household in Kinderhook District, Washington Co., Virginia, keeping house,
born in Tennessee to Tennessee-born parents, unable to read or
write.[^census_1880_booher] Her household includes Jacob H. Booher, 19, her
stepson from that Booher marriage, and Elizabeth Samms, 16, her own daughter —
a younger sister for Harriet, born about 1863–64. It explains why Harriet was
known locally as "Harriet Booher."[^census_1880_booher] A separate,
apparently unrelated Booher family — James L. Booher, his wife Rebecca, and
their children — lived two households away on the same page.

Jane married a third time on 3 April 1889, in Washington County: to that same
James L. Booher, by then a widower himself, both parties recorded as widowed
and Jane's birthplace given as Sullivan Co., Tennessee — the same county as
Hiram Sams's own enlistment, tying this record firmly to the same
woman.[^wash_co_va_marriage_1889_booher] No record of her first marriage, to
Hiram Sams, has been found; nor has any record naming her first Booher
husband, the father of her stepson Jacob.
